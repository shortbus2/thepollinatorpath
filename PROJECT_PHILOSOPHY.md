# Project Philosophy

> **Purpose:** State the ecological and human values that guide The Pollinator Path.
>
> **Document version:** 1.0.0
>
> **Last updated:** 2026-07-30

## Why the project exists

The Pollinator Path began with a real place and a simple responsibility:

> Take care of your little piece of the world in a way that allows others to thrive alongside you.

A habitat garden changes continuously. Plants establish, move, struggle, and return. Wildlife appears briefly or becomes familiar. Weather reshapes what is possible. Stewardship choices accumulate into habitat. Human memory alone cannot hold every detail, but a database without meaning cannot explain why those details mattered.

The project exists to preserve both.

## Ecological stewardship

Stewardship is not control over a finished landscape. It is an ongoing relationship with soil, water, plants, wildlife, seasons, neighbors, and future conditions.

Documentation supports that relationship by making change visible. A dated observation can reveal what bloomed, who visited, which shelter was used, or how a management choice affected habitat. Over time, careful records help the gardener respond with greater humility and attention.

The project should encourage action that supports life alongside people rather than treating nonhuman life as decoration.

## Beauty and habitat together

An ecologically useful garden does not need to reject beauty. Beauty can invite attention, care, conversation, and participation. Habitat can make beauty deeper by revealing relationships that a purely ornamental landscape might hide.

The project therefore avoids a false choice between aesthetics and ecological function. It asks what is thriving now, what the garden makes possible, and how design can welcome both human visitors and other residents.

## Curiosity is a feature

Curiosity is not an accidental by-product of the interface. It is a design goal.

A useful page may answer a question, but it should also make the reader want to look closer: at a leaf edge, a nesting hole, a seasonal pattern, an uncertain visitor, or a small patch of their own world. “Unknown visitor” can be the beginning of attention rather than a failure.

Technology succeeds when it helps people notice and then quietly gets out of the way.

## Preserve garden history

The garden’s history belongs to the observations made at the time, including uncertainty and incomplete understanding. Later knowledge may refine an identification or add context, but it must not rewrite the original event as though the later conclusion had always been known.

Preservation includes:

- original observations and personal narrative;
- dates, locations, behavior, and conditions;
- photographs and their relationship to observations;
- identification proposals and confidence history;
- plant names, status, movement, and stewardship history; and
- decisions about what became public and why.

History is accumulated through links and revisions, not manufactured by replacement.

## Human meaning belongs

People form relationships with recurring garden residents. Names such as Beth, Brenda, Saul, and Big Booty Judy carry memory and affection. Those names supplement taxonomy; they do not compete with it.

A resident can have a human-readable identity while remaining linked to a cautious scientific classification. The project preserves both because ecological knowledge and human care reinforce one another.

## Why small spaces matter

No single garden solves habitat loss, climate pressure, or declining biodiversity. A small space still matters.

It can provide food, water, shelter, nesting material, passage, and seasonal continuity. It can preserve one observation that would otherwise disappear. It can change what a neighbor notices. It can show that stewardship is possible at a human scale.

The Pollinator Path is not an argument that one garden is exceptional. It is an invitation to recognize that every little piece of the world participates in a larger living system.
